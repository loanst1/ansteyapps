Handwriting and Parkinson's

When your writing gets smaller and smaller

It has a name — micrographia — and it is one of the most common changes people notice in Parkinson's. Letters start out fine and shrink as the line goes on. Signatures stop looking like your signature. Birthday cards get harder to face.

This page has free printable practice sheets, an explanation of what is actually happening, and an app that measures the one thing most handwriting tools miss.

Three things worth doing before you start

  • Sit properly at a table with your feet flat on the floor. Writing on your lap makes everything harder.
  • If the pen is hard to hold, a thicker barrel or a foam grip usually helps more than trying harder.
  • Tilt the paper slightly and hold it with your other hand or a clip.

What micrographia actually is

Two things tend to happen together, and they are worth telling apart.

Consistent micrographia is writing that is simply small throughout — it was smaller last year than the year before, and it stays that size across the page.

Progressive micrographia is the one people describe most vividly: writing that starts at a normal size and gets smaller as the line goes on, so the end of a sentence is half the height of the beginning. It is reported in roughly two thirds of people with Parkinson's who have handwriting changes.

That distinction matters because most handwriting practice — and every scoring app we have seen — only looks at how big a letter is. That can see the first kind. It is blind to the second, which is the kind people actually notice.

The usual advice is to write deliberately large, well past what feels natural, because the internal sense of "normal size" is exactly what has drifted. Practising at an exaggerated amplitude and then checking whether it held to the end of the line is the point of the exercise.

This is general information, not advice about your own condition. Your Parkinson's nurse, neurologist or occupational therapist knows your situation and we do not.
