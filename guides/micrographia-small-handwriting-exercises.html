Micrographia: exercises for small, cramped handwriting in Parkinson’s

Handwriting that gets smaller and tighter is common in Parkinson’s — and practising “big”, with the right cues, can help you keep writing legibly.

What is micrographia?

Micrographia is abnormally small, cramped handwriting. It is a common feature of Parkinson’s disease, and it often gets progressively smaller across a word, line or page as you write. It can appear early, sometimes before other symptoms are obvious, and it makes forms, notes and signatures harder to read.

Why “writing big” helps — even when medication doesn’t

Parkinson’s medication and other treatments can improve the speed and fluency of writing, but they often don’t restore its size — which is exactly what makes handwriting hard to read. This is where practice comes in. Research shows that giving the writer an external cue — a visual target size, lined paper, or a reminder to “write big” — brings the size of writing back toward normal, and the improvement can carry over to free writing afterwards. Larger, whole-arm movement practice (the principle behind “big” movement programmes for Parkinson’s) works on the same idea.

Exercises you can try at home

Check with your Parkinson’s team or an occupational therapist first — these are general techniques.

1. Warm up big

Before writing, make large loops, zig-zags and up-and-down strokes using your whole arm, not just your fingers — on paper or in the air. This primes bigger movements.

2. Use the lines as a size cue

Write on widely-spaced lined or squared paper and aim to fill the space — let each letter reach the lines. The visual boundary is a constant reminder to keep letters tall and open.

3. Deliberately write larger than feels natural

Because writing drifts smaller, consciously over-size your letters. It will feel too big at first; that is usually about right. Think or quietly say “big” as you start each word.

4. Trace over a model

Following a large letter or word before writing it unaided gives your hand a bigger movement path to copy.

Cues fade, so keep practising. Short, regular sessions maintain the effect better than occasional long ones. If your hand tires, rest.

When to get help

Talk to your Parkinson’s specialist, neurologist or occupational therapist — they can review your medication timing and refer you to programmes designed around large-amplitude movement. Mention micrographia specifically, as it responds to targeted practice.

Common questions

Does Parkinson’s medication fix small handwriting?
It often improves speed and fluency but tends not to restore writing size, which is what makes handwriting hard to read — so practice that targets size still helps.
Can micrographia be improved?
Yes — external cues (like lined paper and reminders to write big) and large-movement practice can increase writing size, and the benefit can carry over to free writing.
How often should I practise?
Short, regular sessions work best. Cues fade over time, so keeping it up matters more than long one-off sessions.
