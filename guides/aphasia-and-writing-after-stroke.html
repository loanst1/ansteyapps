Aphasia and writing after stroke: practising at home

Aphasia can change speaking, understanding, reading and writing all at once. Here is how gentle, regular writing practice at home can support the therapy that leads recovery — and how families can help.

What aphasia is

Aphasia is a language disorder caused by injury to the brain — most often a stroke. It can affect speaking, understanding, reading and writing, in any combination and to any degree. It does not affect intelligence: the thoughts, memories and opinions are still there — finding and shaping the words is what has become hard. That distinction matters, because people with aphasia are too often spoken over or spoken down to.

Writing practice is one part of a bigger picture

Aphasia rehabilitation is led by a speech and language therapist (in some countries a speech-language pathologist), who assesses how aphasia affects each person and sets the priorities — which may be speaking, understanding, reading, writing or all four. Writing practice at home is a useful supplement between sessions, not a plan of its own. If you have a therapist, follow their programme first and use the ideas below alongside it.

Practising writing at home

These are general ideas, not a personalised programme — a speech and language therapist can tailor them to you.

1. Copy, cover, write from memory

Write the word out (or have someone write it), copy it while looking, then cover it and write it from memory. Check, and try again. This copy-and-recall pattern gives the word a chance to move from copying into recall — the step writing depends on.

2. Personally relevant words

Practise the words that matter in your life: your name and your family’s names, your address, the pet, the items on this week’s shopping list. Useful words get practised more willingly — and get used, which is the point.

3. Little and often

Ten minutes once or twice a day beats an hour once a week. Language work with aphasia is tiring in a way that surprises people; stop while it still feels doable, and come back later.

4. Celebrate attempts, not just successes

An attempted word — half-spelled, mis-spelled, abandoned halfway — is practice, not failure. Notice and praise the attempt. Confidence decides whether someone keeps writing, and confidence is built from attempts that were met warmly.

Aphasia varies from day to day, and tiredness makes it worse. A difficult day is not lost progress — it is a reason to keep sessions short and stop early.

How families can help

  • Slow down, keep sentences short, and ask one question at a time.
  • Give time to answer — count to ten before helping, and don’t finish sentences uninvited.
  • Welcome every channel: gesture, drawing, pointing and writing all count as communication.
  • Don’t correct every error — respond to what was meant, not how it came out.
  • Keep the person in conversations and decisions. Aphasia affects language, not competence.

When to get help

Any sudden new difficulty with speaking, understanding or writing is an emergency — call emergency services, as it can signal a new stroke. For ongoing support, ask your GP or stroke team for a referral to speech and language therapy. National organisations are worth knowing too: the National Aphasia Association in the US (whose resource directory lists ReWrite) and the Stroke Association in the UK offer information, helplines and local groups.

Common questions

Can writing improve years after a stroke?
Improvement is often possible well beyond the first months, particularly with regular practice, though the pace and extent vary from person to person. A speech and language therapist can advise on what is realistic for you.
Does writing practice replace speech and language therapy?
No. Assessment and treatment of aphasia are led by a speech and language therapist. Home practice — on paper or in an app — supports the work between sessions; it is not therapy in itself.
Which words should we practise first?
Personally relevant ones: names of family and friends, your address, everyday shopping items. Words that matter in your life are more motivating to practise and more useful once relearned.
