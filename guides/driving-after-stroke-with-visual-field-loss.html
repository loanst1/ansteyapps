Driving after stroke with a visual field loss: what to know

If a stroke has affected your field of vision, whether you can drive is a legal and medical question — here is how it works and how to find out safely.

Why a visual field loss matters for driving

Driving needs a wide, reliable field of view to spot hazards, pedestrians and vehicles approaching from the side. A stroke that causes hemianopia (loss of half the field), quadrantanopia (a quarter) or visual neglect can leave a gap on one side that makes driving unsafe — even when central vision for reading a number plate is fine. Neglect is especially important, because a person may not be aware of what they are missing.

It is a legal and medical decision — not one to make alone

Most countries set a legal visual field standard for driving, and most require you to notify the licensing authority of a stroke or a new field loss. The rules, the exact field standard, and any waiting period vary from place to place, so the essential step is to check with your own licensing authority and an eye-care professional (optometrist, orthoptist or ophthalmologist) before you drive again. Do not rely on how your vision feels — a formal visual field test is what counts.

Can people return to driving?

Some do. After the early recovery period, and depending on the type and extent of the field loss, some people meet the legal standard or qualify through an on-road or specialist assessment; others do not and find alternatives. An honest assessment protects you and everyone else on the road.

Where scanning practice fits in

Compensatory scanning training — building the habit of deliberately looking into the affected side — can improve how well you detect things around you and is part of many rehabilitation programmes. It can help your everyday safety and may support driving assessment, but it does not remove the legal requirement to meet the field standard or to be assessed. Treat it as building skill, not as a substitute for clearance.

Never resume driving on the assumption that it is probably fine. Notify the licensing authority, get the visual field tested, and follow the decision you are given.

When to get help

Ask your GP, stroke team, orthoptist or neuro-optometrist to map your visual field and advise on driving. They can explain the standard where you live, arrange a formal field test, and point you to specialist driving assessment services if appropriate. Seek urgent advice for any sudden new vision change.

Common questions

Can I drive with hemianopia after a stroke?
It depends on the legal visual field standard where you live and on assessment. Homonymous hemianopia often does not meet the standard, but some people qualify through specialist assessment. Notify your licensing authority and get a formal field test before driving.
Do I have to tell the licensing authority about my stroke?
In most places, yes — you are legally required to declare a stroke or a new visual field loss. Check the rules with your own licensing authority; not declaring can have legal and insurance consequences.
Can scanning training let me drive again?
It can improve how well you detect hazards and may support a driving assessment, but it does not by itself restore eligibility. Meeting the legal field standard, or passing a specialist assessment, is what determines whether you can drive.
